# One

*Proof-of-concept draft. Covers W1, Thursday to Friday. Close third person, past tense. Both choices are provisional.*

---

*Sailing By* came on at a quarter to one, as it did every night, and Adam Rennick lay in the dark and waited for the voice.

He had not slept without the shipping forecast since he was nine. As a boy he'd found it on his father's radio, a heavy long-wave set with a dial that glowed the colour of weak tea, and after his father left he had gone on finding it, night after night. It wasn't comfort exactly. It was a list. The sea was divided into areas, every area had a name, and every name was read out in the same order, whatever the weather was doing to the people out there in it. *Viking, North Utsire, South Utsire.* Nothing was left out. Nothing was read out of turn.

The long-wave set was on the kitchen windowsill. It had been silent since June, when the BBC switched long wave off, and he had moved the forecast to the small digital radio by his bed. He hadn't forgiven anyone for this. He knew that was unreasonable, and he left it unforgiven anyway.

*And now the shipping forecast, issued by the Met Office on behalf of the Maritime and Coastguard Agency at double-oh-fifteen today.*

*Forth, Tyne, Dogger.* Westerly five or six. Showers. Good, occasionally moderate.

His mother had been dead for nine weeks. He knew it was nine because he had been counting, the way you count stairs in the dark: not to arrive anywhere, just to know where you are.

---

The claim on Thursday morning was a burst pipe in a ground-floor flat in Gorgie. The claimant, Mrs Ferrier, was seventy-three, and she had kept the kettle on for him.

"It went on Tuesday," she said. "I came through in the morning and the whole floor was swimming."

Adam said that must have been a shock. He said it sincerely, and wrote *Tues (claimant)* in his notebook. Then he knelt by the skirting board under the sink and looked at the damage, which had its own account of things.

The chipboard under the vinyl had swollen and started to crumble at the edges. Chipboard does that slowly. There was a tide line on the plaster about four inches up, and above it a second, fainter one, where the water had risen, dried and risen again. In the corner behind the bin, a bloom of mould had gone from grey to green to that soft black that looks like velvet. It doesn't get there in two days. It takes a week, more likely ten days.

Over eleven years Adam had come to think of damage as the most honest witness he would ever interview. It didn't want anything. It couldn't be embarrassed. Water went where it went and left a record of how long it had stayed.

He sat back on his heels. "Were you away at all, Mrs Ferrier? In the last couple of weeks?"

There was a pause, a little too long. Then she said, "My sister's. In Dunfermline. Only a few days."

"How many days?"

"Nine." She said it to the kettle. "She'd had a result back from the hospital. I didn't think. You don't think, do you, you just go."

The policy would have an unoccupancy clause, as policies did. A property left empty for more than a set number of days lost certain cover. A man in a different mood, or in a different job, would have written *nine days, unoccupied* and let the clause do the rest.

Adam wrote: *Claimant attending family medical emergency. Escape of water discovered on return.* All of it was true. Then he asked about her sister. They talked for twenty minutes, which was not on the form, about consultants and waiting lists and how nobody tells you anything. He knew a fair amount about that by now.

It happened as he was leaving, with his hand on the door.

He was looking at the coat hooks in her hall, three brass hooks and a tartan scarf. Then he was still looking at them, and Mrs Ferrier was saying, "…so I said to her, you'll have to tell him yourself." He didn't know who *him* was. He hadn't heard the start of the sentence. The light in the hall hadn't changed and nobody had moved. He'd simply lost a stretch of time, the length of a sentence or maybe two, as if he'd blinked and the blink had lasted too long.

"Quite right," he said. He'd been saying *quite right* into gaps like this since he was nine years old, and he was good at it.

In the car he sat with his hands on the wheel and didn't start the engine.

The doctors had called them absences when he was small. His mother had called it *going away*: *he goes away sometimes, don't you, love*. There had been tests, an overnight stay at the Sick Kids, wires glued to his scalp, a kind woman with a radio. Afterwards he was told he would grow out of it, and to a large extent he had. He couldn't remember the last one. Two years ago? Three? He had never told anyone about them as an adult. There had never been anyone it seemed worth telling. Or there had been once, and he hadn't told her either.

He started the engine. The damage would wait. Damage always did.

---

The fridge died at about half past eight.

He noticed it the way you notice a clock that has stopped. He was halfway through a sentence in his diary, and the kitchen went from quiet to *silent*. He looked up. The hum he had stopped hearing years ago had gone, and in its absence the room sounded bigger and a little shocked.

He opened the door. The light stayed off. The milk was still cold, but it wouldn't be for long.

It was a tall, narrow, ugly fridge, the only one that would fit the space between the boiler cupboard and the wall. He and Nell had bought it together, in the second year, from a man in Leith who swore it was nearly new. *Nearly new*, Nell said for months afterwards, about anything that was obviously falling apart. *Nearly new*, about his jumper, his car, his excuses.

He unplugged it at the wall and plugged it in again, which did nothing, and he knew it would do nothing. Then he took hold of it by the top corners and walked it out of its slot, one side and then the other, the way you move a wardrobe.

Behind it, the wall was tiled to shoulder height, like the rest of the kitchen. The tiles were white, and on every fifth one there was a small blue swallow, wings back, going somewhere in a hurry. They had been here when he viewed the flat. He had stood in the doorway, seen them, and known before he looked at the bedroom, the boiler or the survey that he was going to buy the place, which had alarmed him, because it wasn't how he made decisions. Nell had wanted to retile. *Your swallows*, she called them. It started as a joke and then it stopped being one.

On the floor in the gap was eight years of what falls behind a fridge: dust gone grey and soft, a teaspoon, a fridge magnet from a pizza place that had closed before the pandemic, a dried husk of something that had once been a grape.

And an earring.

He picked it up. It was a drop of green glass the size of a fingernail, on a thin gold hook, the green of a wine bottle held up to a window. The hook was slightly bent, as if it had been pulled out in a hurry.

He turned it over under the kitchen light, and the light went through it and made a small green coin on his palm.

It had to be Nell's. Nobody else had lived here. Nobody else had been in this kitchen often enough or long enough to leave an earring behind the fridge. Nell had worn earrings every day. And he had paid attention to her earrings, because paying attention was the one thing he was reliably good at. Silver hoops. The little jet studs that had been her grandmother's. A pair of enamel lemons she wore to make people smile.

He had never seen this one in his life.

He stood there for a while. Then, because it was the kind of thing he did, he went through the alternatives as he would have at work. A friend of hers, at a dinner? They hadn't really had dinners. A cleaner? They'd never had one. Something from before he bought the flat, the previous owner's? But the previous owner had retiled the kitchen, and the fridge space had been empty until he and Nell filled it. So it came from after the fridge. It came from them.

He went into the hall and put it in the pocket of his coat, which was hanging by the door, so that he wouldn't forget it. He kept his hand closed around it in the pocket for a moment, as if it might try to leave.

That was when he knew.

It wasn't a thought. Thoughts had a shape he could check. This had no shape at all. It was simply there, the way a step is there under your foot in the dark: *he was going to lose this tomorrow.* He was going to lose it, and he wouldn't feel it go.

He took his hand out of the pocket and looked at it, the ordinary hand of a forty-one-year-old man, with a paper cut on the side of one finger from Mrs Ferrier's claim form.

*Ridiculous*, he thought, carefully, the way you'd put a word down on a table between two people.

He was tired. He had been tired for nine weeks, and for some time before that, during the months of hospital car parks. Grief made you superstitious. He knew that and had read it: people in mourning see patterns, hear voices, feel certain of things, because the brain doesn't like a door that has been slammed and keeps going back to try the handle. He had found something of Nell's in the week he was finding things of his mother's, and his mind had made a little drama out of it. That was all it was.

But.

He was not a man who ignored a feeling just because he couldn't account for it. That wasn't what being careful meant. Being careful meant this: when you couldn't tell whether the evidence was false or your model was wrong, you didn't pick one. You arranged things so that it didn't matter.

If he posted it, it might get lost. Posted things got lost. If he left it on the side to deal with at the weekend, it would sit there for a month and slowly become another object he couldn't decide about, like the box of his mother's letters in the boot of his car. If he believed in the feeling, the safe thing was to put it in an envelope and in a drawer and not take it anywhere tomorrow. If he didn't believe in the feeling, it didn't matter what he did.

The thing he actually wanted to do, he realised, was neither of these. He wanted to give it back to her. In person. Tomorrow.

He noticed this and didn't like what it implied. They hadn't spoken in four years. The last thing she'd said to him was *Then I'll go*, standing in the kitchen doorway with her bag. She'd gone, and he had stayed. That was the shape of it, and he'd lived inside that shape long enough to know where all its walls were.

He picked up his phone and wrote to her sister, because he didn't have the nerve to write to Nell and he'd never had the nerve to lie to Jo.

*Jo, sorry, I know this is out of nowhere. I found something of Nell's in the flat. I'd like to give it back to her rather than post it. Is she still in Leith? I won't stay.*

He read it four times and sent it. Then he went back into the kitchen and finished his diary entry, which was three lines long and contained the fridge, the claim and the weather, but not the earring. He couldn't think how to write the earring down without writing down the other thing as well.

The reply came at twenty past ten.

*Ferry Road end of Leith Walk. 2F2, the one with the green door. She's not expecting you.*

And then, a minute later:

*Be kind.*

He read that more times than the first message. He'd never been unkind to Nell, not once. He had been careful with her the way he was careful with everything. He thought about writing *I was always kind*, and he could hear exactly how Jo would read it.

He wrote *I will. Thanks*, and lay in the dark until *Sailing By* came on and the voice read the sea back into order.

---

He kept checking all day on Friday.

He checked it in the car on the way to a fire claim in Dalkeith. He checked it while the fire officer showed him where the chip pan had been. He checked it in the queue at Greggs, and while he was on the phone to the office. Each time his fingers found the little cold drop of glass and the thin wire of the hook, and each time he felt foolish, and each time he checked again twenty minutes later.

He thought about leaving it in the flat. He'd thought about it at breakfast, with the coat in his hand. That would have been the sensible thing, if he believed in the feeling. But he didn't believe in it. And if he didn't believe in it, then leaving it at home only proved that he did. He'd gone round this loop twice, then put the coat on and gone to work, which had felt at the time like a decision.

He left the office at five. The rain had come in off the Forth in the late afternoon, the fine sideways rain that doesn't so much fall on Edinburgh as hang there waiting for people. He parked off Pilrig Street and walked down Leith Walk with his collar up and his hand in his pocket.

He knew this road. He'd walked it with Nell a hundred times, to the Polish deli, to the bar with the terrible quiz, to the bus stop on the nights she had an early start. The shops had changed. The light hadn't.

The earring was there at the junction with Iona Street. He knew because he checked.

He crossed. A bus came up behind him and threw up a sheet of gutter water, and he stepped back onto the kerb to avoid it, and the bus went past, lit and steamed up, with nobody looking out.

He put his hand in his pocket, and it wasn't there.

His fingers went all the way to the bottom of the pocket and through it. There was a hole in the lining, the size of a fingertip, at the seam. He must have had it for years. He had no memory of ever noticing it, and his fingers had never found it until now.

Afterwards, he would try very hard to remember what he felt in that moment, because it was the most important evidence he had, and he was a professional. And what he felt was not surprise.

It was recognition. It was the feeling of a tune arriving that you didn't know you knew, where you have the next note in your mouth before it's played. *Yes*, something in him said, quietly and without any triumph at all. *That's right. That's the one.*

Then the ordinary panic came, and he was glad of it.

He went back over the last twenty yards bent double, with his phone torch on, in the rain. People stepped round him. A woman asked if he'd dropped his keys. He said, "An earring," and she looked at his ears and moved on. He searched the kerb, the gutter and the base of the bus stop. By the kerb there was a drain grating, black iron bars with black water running between them fast and full, taking the street away somewhere under the city. He crouched over it with the light and saw nothing but his own torch looking back.

He stood up. His knees were soaked. It was 17:43 by his phone, and he noted it automatically, as he would have done at work.

He had not felt it go.

He stood on Leith Walk in the rain for a long minute and made himself think it through properly, because if he didn't, he was going to start thinking it through improperly.

He'd had a feeling. The feeling was specific: tomorrow, and he wouldn't feel it go. The feeling had come true. That was one fact.

Here was another. A man who is certain he is going to lose something will check his pocket fifty times in a day. A man who checks his pocket fifty times in a day will work at a small hole in the lining, will get his fingers into it, will widen it. The certainty had produced the checking. The checking had produced the loss. It was a closed system. There was nothing in it that needed anything stranger than nerves.

That was an explanation, and a good one. He would have accepted it from a claimant, and probably written it up with some sympathy.

He noticed that he didn't feel any better.

The sensible thing now was to go home. He had nothing to give her. He had promised Jo he wouldn't stay, and he was in no state to stay. He could write. He could say he was sorry, that he'd found something of hers and lost it again, and she could make of that whatever she liked.

He walked on down Leith Walk to the Ferry Road end, and found the green door.

---

The buzzer for 2F2 said IMRIE in Nell's handwriting, the capitals leaning forward as if they were late for something. He pressed it before he could rehearse anything, and that was a mistake, because then the intercom crackled and he hadn't rehearsed anything.

"Hello?"

"Nell. It's Adam."

A long silence. The rain filled it.

"Jo said you might," she said. "I told her you wouldn't."

The door buzzed.

She met him on the landing of the second floor, not in the doorway of the flat. He understood that this was a decision. She was smaller than his memory of her. Maybe he'd been remembering her angry, and anger took up more room. Her hair was shorter. She had a cardigan on over her work clothes, and there was soil under two of her fingernails, the way there always used to be. That nearly finished him, there on the stair, more than anything she might have said.

"You're soaking," she said.

"It's raining."

"I'm aware it's raining, Adam." There was nearly something in her voice. Not warmth, but its outline, the place where warmth had been. "What's this about? Jo said you'd found something."

"I did. I found an earring. Behind the fridge. The fridge died, and I moved it, and it was there." He was giving her the facts in order. He could hear himself doing it. "I wanted to give it back to you."

She held out her hand, palm up. The gesture was so simple and so reasonable that it hurt.

"I lost it," he said. "On the way here. On Leith Walk, about ten minutes ago. There's a hole in my pocket."

She looked at him. Then she laughed, one short breath through her nose with nothing kind in it.

"You came all the way down Leith Walk in the rain," she said, "after four years, to tell me you'd lost something of mine."

"Yes. I know how it sounds."

"I don't think you do."

He should have left then. He could see the moment for leaving, clear as a door, and he could see himself not taking it, and he couldn't understand why, any more than he'd understood why he walked the last half mile with nothing in his pocket. He said, "It was green glass. A drop. On a gold hook. I'd never seen it before."

Nell's face changed.

He was good at faces. Claimants' faces, at the moment the damage contradicted them. Faces when the version they'd been telling met the version on the wall. This wasn't that. This was something going still, all the way down, the way water goes still before it freezes.

"No," she said quietly. "You wouldn't have."

"Then it's not yours?"

"It's mine." She wasn't looking at him now. She was looking at the stairwell window, at the rain running down it. "I bought them that afternoon. A pair. In that shop on Raeburn Place that's gone now. I wanted to look nice. I thought if I looked nice it wouldn't feel like an ambush." She stopped. "I lost one that night. I didn't notice till I got to Jo's."

"That night," he said.

"That Thursday." She looked back at him, and her eyes were perfectly dry, and he'd have given a great deal for them not to be. "The night you ended it."

The stairwell light, on a timer, clicked off. Neither of them moved to press it. In the dark, with the rain loud on the window, he said the thing that had been the floor under him for four years, the one fact he had never had to check.

"The night you left."

He heard her breathe in. He waited for her to argue, to tell him what had happened, and he realised with a kind of vertigo that he was waiting to hear her version as if it were evidence. As if he could set it against his own and see which one the damage agreed with.

She didn't argue. She reached past him and pressed the light, and in the sudden yellow she looked tired, and older, and exactly like herself.

"Goodnight, Adam," she said. "Thank you for not bringing me my earring."

The door of 2F2 closed quietly. It was worse than if she'd slammed it.

---

He wrote it down that night, because he wrote everything down.

*Fri. Fire claim, Dalkeith (chip pan, kitchen only, no structural). Rain from approx. 16:00.*

*N's earring (green glass, gold hook) lost Leith Walk nr Iona St approx. 17:40. Hole in coat pocket lining. Did not feel it go.*

*Saw N. 2 mins. She says she bought the earrings that day. She says*

He stopped. He sat with the pen above the page for a long time, while the kitchen made its new silent noise and the swallows went nowhere on the wall behind the dead fridge.

He wrote: *She says I ended it.*

Then, underneath, in smaller letters, because it was true, and because a record with a known omission is no longer a record:

*I knew I would lose it. I knew on Thursday.*

He looked at the line for a while. Then he crossed it out, neatly, with a single straight stroke, so that it could still be read.

At a quarter to one *Sailing By* came on, and he lay in the dark waiting for the voice to put the sea back in order. Tonight, for the first time he could remember, it didn't.
